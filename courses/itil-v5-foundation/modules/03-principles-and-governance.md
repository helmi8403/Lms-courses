# 3. Guiding principles and governance

## Learning goals

Apply principles to a decision rather than reciting them. Distinguish governance from day-to-day management and show how decisions connect to value.

## 3.1 Seven principles in everyday decisions

**Focus on value.** Begin with who benefits and what they need. Harbour should prioritize reliable assessment submission over an ornamental dashboard if submission failures prevent course completion. Include indirect stakeholders and longer-term consequences.

**Start where you are.** Inspect existing capability before replacing it. Interview support staff and examine usable knowledge. Do not assume everything is broken, but do not preserve ineffective work simply because it already exists.

**Progress iteratively with feedback.** Break work into useful, manageable increments and learn from each. Pilot a new onboarding flow with a representative group, evaluate actual completion and accessibility, then adapt. A small increment must still be safe and valuable.

**Collaborate and promote visibility.** Involve relevant people and make work understandable. Show queue size and decision ownership so teams can coordinate. Visibility does not mean exposing confidential learner records to everyone.

**Think and work holistically.** Improve the whole service. A faster development team can worsen the total result if support receives unmanageable releases. Consider dependencies and the customer journey, not only local throughput.

**Keep it simple and practical.** Use the minimum complexity that reliably achieves the purpose. Remove steps that add neither value nor necessary control. Simplicity is not an excuse to omit a required security or accessibility check.

**Optimize and automate.** Understand and improve an activity before automating it. Automating an unnecessary approval queue produces a faster unnecessary queue. Where automation is appropriate, retain observability, ownership and a response to failure.

Principles reinforce each other. Starting from current evidence supports iterative work; collaboration makes that evidence richer; holistic thinking prevents misleading local optimization. A useful answer explains the interaction rather than selecting one slogan.

## 3.2 Governance and the Value System

Governance concerns the direction, oversight and accountability by which an organization is guided. Management translates direction into plans and activities. A board or accountable leadership group might set acceptable risk and investment priorities; delivery teams decide how to achieve outcomes within those boundaries.

The Value System brings together principles, governance, management practices, value-creating activity and continual improvement. Its purpose is coherence: opportunities and demand should lead to valuable results, not disconnected departmental projects.

For Harbour, a governing decision might prioritize trustworthy assessment over rapid enrollment growth. Management then adjusts the roadmap, service measures, supplier expectations and improvement backlog. If teams are still rewarded only for signups, the system is inconsistent even if a policy document is excellent.

## 3.3 Evidence and accountability

A decision record should explain the outcome sought, options considered, accountable owner, constraints, evidence, risks, decision and review date. It should not be a ritual form with empty fields. Make decisions traceable at a level proportionate to their consequences.

Controls can enable work by reducing ambiguity. Pre-agreed low-risk change conditions may allow routine work to proceed quickly, while high-impact changes receive appropriate review. Governance is not the same as requiring every decision to wait for the most senior person.

## Exercise

Harbour proposes rebuilding every service because a new platform is fashionable. The current enrollment service works but assessment submission fails frequently. Recommend an approach using at least three principles and distinguish a governance decision from a management action.

**Model reasoning:** Focus on learner outcomes; inspect the existing assessment capability; improve in manageable increments and evaluate feedback. Governance clarifies the risk appetite and investment objective. Management investigates causes, chooses an implementation approach and measures whether submission success improves. “Approve the rebuild” is not evidence-based solely because the platform is new.
