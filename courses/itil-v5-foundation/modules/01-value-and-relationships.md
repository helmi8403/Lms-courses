# 1. Orientation, value and relationships

## Learning goals

Explain what management is trying to achieve; distinguish a product from a service, output from outcome, and utility from warranty; describe an offering and the responsibilities within a service relationship.

## 1.1 Start with the purpose, not a tool

Harbour Learning wants working adults to finish useful courses despite irregular schedules. A working video player is an output. A learner being able to complete a lesson on a slow connection and apply a skill at work is closer to an outcome. The organization needs both, but an output alone does not demonstrate value.

A digital product combines resources and capabilities that can support offerings. A service helps a consumer achieve outcomes while the provider takes responsibility for some associated costs and risks. Neither means the consumer has no responsibilities. Learners still need time, suitable devices and appropriate use of the service.

Management means making decisions about priorities, resources, performance and improvement. A service-management framework provides a shared way to reason about those decisions. It is not a requirement to install a particular ticketing system or create bureaucracy for every action.

## 1.2 A working vocabulary

**Value** depends on the stakeholder and situation. Convenience for a learner may coincide with increased workload for an instructor. Ask whose value is being discussed, and over what time horizon.

**Outcome** is the result a stakeholder wants. **Output** is a produced deliverable. Harbour might output a certificate, but the intended outcome is credible evidence of competence. Counting certificates without checking competence can reward the wrong behavior.

**Cost** includes money, time, effort and opportunity cost. **Risk** concerns uncertainty that can affect objectives. Outsourcing video delivery may reduce infrastructure work while introducing dependence on a supplier.

**Utility** concerns whether the service does what it is needed for. **Warranty** concerns whether it can be relied on under the agreed conditions. Offline playback can add utility; dependable availability, security and sufficient capacity support warranty. A feature-rich application that repeatedly fails during exams has not succeeded simply because its feature list is long.

**Quality** should be evaluated against requirements and stakeholder needs, not an attractive interface alone. **Experience** includes the sequence of interactions before, during and after use. **Sustainability** asks whether benefits can endure without unacceptable economic, environmental or social costs. These lenses may expose tradeoffs that a single availability figure misses.

## 1.3 Design an offering

An offering describes what a consumer can obtain. It may combine access to resources, service activities and transferred goods. Harbour's supported course offering includes access to learning content, instructor feedback, account assistance and assessment arrangements. The software alone is not the full offering.

Write an offering in language a consumer can understand:

- Intended consumers and their needs.
- Outcomes it supports and limits it does not remove.
- What is included and excluded.
- Access conditions, support arrangements and expected performance.
- Responsibilities of provider, consumer and suppliers.
- Costs, constraints and how feedback is handled.

Avoid promising “24/7 support” if the actual capability is a help form collected at any hour but answered next business day. Receiving a request is not the same as resolving it.

## 1.4 Relationships and co-creation

A customer helps define requirements and takes responsibility for the results of consuming the service. A user interacts with it. A sponsor authorizes or supplies funding. One person can occupy several roles; the distinction helps uncover conflicting expectations.

At Harbour, a corporate training manager is the customer, employees are users, and a business-unit director funds the program. The users may value short lessons while the sponsor values measurable compliance. Co-creation means those perspectives influence design, feedback and use, not that the provider can delegate every problem back to consumers.

Relationship management is ongoing. Before launch, establish expectations; during use, collect feedback and manage commitments; afterward, assess outcomes and improve or retire the offering. A supplier relationship needs equivalent clarity about responsibilities and evidence.

## Exercise

A dashboard says: “1,000 accounts created; all servers online; satisfaction falling.” Identify two possible gaps between outputs and outcomes. Draft one offering statement and one question for each of customer, user and sponsor.

**Model reasoning:** Accounts and server uptime do not show that learners can find relevant content or complete activities. Ask users about completion barriers; customers about whether requirements were met; sponsors about the result that justifies investment. A suitable offering statement names the learning result and access/support limits rather than merely listing software features.

## Check yourself

Can a service have strong utility but weak warranty? Yes: useful capabilities can be unreliable. Can value differ across stakeholders? Yes: measure and negotiate tradeoffs rather than assuming a universal benefit. Is a service relationship finished when a contract is signed? No: expectations, feedback and performance need continuing attention.
